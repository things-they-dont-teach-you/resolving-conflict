# Lesson 2: Listening properly
<!-- facts: Active listening -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Say why people keep repeating themselves in an argument, and how to stop it.
- Use the six active-listening skills: encourage, question, restate, reflect, summarise, validate.
- Check they've understood before they reply.
- Tell the difference between understanding someone and agreeing with them.

## Key ideas

**Most arguments are two people waiting to talk.** While the other person speaks, we plan our
answer. They can tell, so they repeat themselves, louder. Feeling heard often calms a conflict
down before anything is solved.

**The six skills** (`facts.md`):

- **Encourage:** nods, "go on", "mm". Show you want to hear more.
- **Question:** open questions that start with what, how or tell me: "What happened then?"
- **Restate:** the facts in your own words. "So the rent went up and nobody told you."
- **Reflect:** the feeling behind it. "That sounds really frustrating."
- **Summarise:** pull it together. "So the main things are the noise and the bills."
- **Validate:** "That makes sense, given what you'd been told." Not "you're right".

**Check, then reply.** Ask "Have I got that right?" and let them correct you. Only when they say
yes do you give your side. It feels slow. It's usually faster.

**Understanding is not agreeing.** You can reflect someone's anger accurately and still disagree
with what they want. Listening first makes them far more likely to listen to you.

**What gets in the way:** interrupting, jumping to advice, "at least...", turning it into your
own story, and checking your phone.

## Worked example

Sam's flatmate Jordan snaps: "You never clean up after your friends come over." Sam's first
thought is "That's not true." Instead: "Sounds like you've been left with the mess a few times.
Is it the kitchen mostly?" Jordan: "Yes, and I had an early start." Sam: "So you came home tired
to a messy kitchen. That's fair to be annoyed about." Jordan's voice drops. Now they can talk
about what to do.

## Role-play

The tutor plays an upset friend or flatmate with a complaint about the learner (made up, and a
little unfair at first). The learner may not defend themselves until they've restated, reflected
and summarised, and the tutor has said "Yes, that's it." Then they give their side. Debrief:
which skills did they use, and when did the tutor start to calm down?
