# Lesson 6: At home and at work
<!-- facts: Raising a problem at work; Escalation and the four horsemen -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Adapt the skills to family, partners, flatmates and landlords.
- Raise a problem with a colleague or their boss, informally first.
- Say when a work problem becomes a formal or legal matter, and where to get help.

## Key ideas

**Family.** Old roles come back. Stick to one present-day issue, not the history. Agreeing to
differ is a valid outcome.

**Partners.** Small repairs ("Sorry, that came out wrong") matter more than big talks. Watch for
contempt; appreciation is its antidote (`facts.md`). Being afraid of your partner is not a
conflict; see lesson 7.

**Flatmates.** Most fights are about money, cleaning, noise and guests. Agree the rules early,
in writing, before anything goes wrong, and review them.

**Landlords.** Keep it businesslike and in writing (lesson 5). Your lease and local law set the
rules, not who argues best.

**Colleagues.** Talk to the person privately first, with an I-statement. Many problems are
misunderstandings about who does what.

**Your boss.** There's a power gap, so prepare more. Ask for a meeting, bring specifics, and
suggest a solution. Agree next steps and follow up in writing.

**When talking doesn't work** (a good model from Acas, the UK's workplace advice service, in
`facts.md`): check your workplace's policy, raise it informally, get support (HR, a union rep),
try mediation for relationship problems, then raise a formal complaint (a grievance) in writing.
Keep a dated record of what happened.

**When it becomes a legal matter.** Unpaid wages, unfair dismissal, discrimination, harassment
or unsafe work are not just communication problems; you may have legal rights, and deadlines may
apply. The law differs by country. In South Africa, see the `south-african-rights-at-work`
course. Elsewhere, look for your government's labour or employment advice service, a trade
union, or an employment lawyer. Mediation is meant for relationship problems, not for these.

## Worked example

Sam's manager keeps giving him tasks at 4:55pm. He asks for a short meeting: "When work arrives
just before five, I often stay late without notice, and I've had to cancel plans twice. Could
we agree that anything after four goes to the next morning unless it's urgent?" The manager
agrees to try it. Sam sends a two-line email confirming.

## Role-play

The tutor plays a busy boss or a colleague who takes credit for the learner's work. The learner
asks for a meeting, raises it with an I-statement and specifics, listens to the reply, and agrees
a next step. Then they write the confirming email together. Debrief: was it specific, calm, and
on record?
