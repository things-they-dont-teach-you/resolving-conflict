# Lesson 7: Boundaries, walking away, and when it isn't a conflict
<!-- facts: What counts as abuse; Abuse help; Emergency numbers -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Set a boundary: say what they will do, not what the other person must do.
- Decide when a conflict isn't worth more effort, and step back from it.
- Recognise abuse, and know it is not a conflict to resolve.
- Find help for themselves or a friend.

## Key ideas

![Is this a conflict or abuse? Are you afraid of them? Do they hurt or threaten you, control your money, phone or friends, or punish you for saying no? If yes, it isn't a conflict to fix: get help. If no, it's a disagreement between equals, and the skills in this course can help](../diagrams/07-conflict-or-abuse.svg)

**A boundary is about you.** "If you shout, I'll leave the room and we can talk later" is a
boundary: you control it. "You must stop shouting" is a demand: they control it. Say it calmly,
once, then do what you said. People who respect you will respect it, even if they grumble.

**Walking away is allowed.** Some conflicts aren't worth winning, and some people won't meet you
halfway however well you communicate. If a deal is worse than your alternative (lesson 4), you
can leave the flat, the job, the friendship or the shop. That's a choice, not a failure.

**Abuse is not a conflict.** In a conflict, two equals disagree. In abuse, one person uses fear,
force or control over the other. WHO counts physical, sexual and psychological harm and
controlling behaviour (`facts.md`). Warning signs: you feel afraid; they threaten or hurt you;
they control your money, phone, friends or movements; they punish you for setting boundaries.

**Better communication won't fix abuse,** and trying to "talk it through" can make things more
dangerous. It is never the fault of the person being abused. Anyone can be affected, whatever
their gender or background.

**Getting help.** The help lines and emergency numbers in `facts.md` are free and confidential;
you can call for yourself or to ask how to help someone else. If someone is in danger right now,
call the emergency number. Use a device the other person can't check.

**Helping a friend.** Listen, believe them, don't judge or push them to leave, and share the help
line. Specialist services can help them plan for their safety.

## Worked example

Tom's sister keeps borrowing money and not paying it back. He says: "I'm not lending money any
more. I'm happy to help in other ways." When she pushes, he repeats it calmly. She sulks for a
week, then they're fine. That's a boundary. If she had threatened him, it would be a different
lesson: getting help.

## Role-play

The tutor plays a friend or relative who keeps asking for a favour the learner doesn't want to
do. The learner sets a boundary in one or two sentences and holds it twice, calmly, without
over-explaining. The tutor never plays an abusive person. Debrief: did they say what they would
do, rather than what the other person must do?
